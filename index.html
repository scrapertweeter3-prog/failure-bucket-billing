Every failing token still lands on the same bill

Support teams grade an assistant by its ticket outcomes, and the scoreboard splits an agent month into three buckets: 12,400 sessions end in a clean resolution, 2,900 stall partway and get picked up by a human, and 700 run to the end and still leave the customer angry. Success rate gets reported as 12,400 of 16,000, about 77 percent, and that number reads like a discount. A vendor pitching a stronger model at $2.40 per million input tokens against the current $0.80 can sound cheap, because the instinct is to price the model that fixed the gap.

Run the bill before the upgrade. One session carries an 11,000-token context, so a call at $0.80/M costs $0.0088 and at $2.40/M costs $0.0264. The clean bucket books $109.12 a month. The stalled 2,900 still consumed their context before the human took over, another $25.52. The angry 700 also ran to their final token, $6.16. Nothing in the failure column reduces the invoice: $140.80 is the whole month, and 22.5 percent of it, $31.68, bought sessions the scoreboard calls failures.

The model swap does not change that arithmetic, it re-prices it. Move all 16,000 sessions to the $2.40 model and the same month costs $422.40. To break even against $140.80 the new model has to eliminate 66.7 percent of all sessions, not the 22.5 percent in the failure buckets, because a clean session costs the same whether the model was smart or lucky.

Routing the hard cases instead of swapping the fleet loses the same way. Send the 3,600 failing sessions to the $2.40 model and keep the 12,400 clean ones on $0.80, and the month is $109.12 plus $95.04, $204.16, a 45 percent premium over $140.80 for identical token volume. The premium buys nothing on this invoice. It only pays if the strong model converts enough stalls into self-serve resolutions to shrink the human queue, and sizing that conversion is an eval measurement, not a line item.
